4G LTE/EPC Architecture

Understanding how LTE works means understanding where the subscriber is, where the session is, where the data is flowing, and which network element is responsible for each decision.

A mobile network is not a collection of boxes. It is a chain of decisions, identities, and tunnels.

In the previous chapters, we built the foundations required to understand IP networking and the general evolution of mobile networks. Now it is time to enter the architecture that made mobile broadband a reality at global scale: the 4G LTE Evolved Packet System.

LTE is often introduced through a list of network elements: UE, eNodeB, MME, SGW, PGW, HSS and PCRF. That list is useful, but it is not enough.

Knowing what the acronyms mean does not mean that you understand the network.

The real understanding begins when you can follow a subscriber from the moment the device appears on the radio network, through authentication and session establishment, until an IP packet reaches the Internet — and then follow the same packet back to the device.

The goal is not to memorize the EPC. The goal is to be able to look at a packet capture, a signaling trace, or an outage and reconstruct what the network is doing.

The LTE Network as an End-to-End System

The LTE architecture separates responsibilities that were historically more tightly coupled. Radio access, mobility management, subscriber data, policy, and packet forwarding become distinct functions.

At the highest level, the architecture can be visualized like this:

LTE RADIO ACCESS +---------------------+ | UE | | User Equipment | +----------+----------+ | | LTE-Uu | +----------v----------+ | eNodeB | | LTE Base Station | +-----+----------+----+ | | S1-MME S1-U | | v v +---------+ +---------+ | MME | | SGW | +----+----+ +----+----+ | | S6a S5/S8 | | v v +---------+ +---------+ | HSS | | PGW | +---------+ +----+----+ | Gi | v IP Networks / Internet

This diagram immediately reveals one of the most important characteristics of EPC: control signaling and user traffic do not necessarily follow the same path.

The MME can be heavily involved in establishing a subscriber's connectivity without ever carrying the subscriber's actual Internet traffic.

Likewise, the SGW and PGW can carry enormous volumes of user-plane traffic while depending on control-plane signaling to create and maintain the tunnels through which that traffic flows.

The control plane decides what should happen. The user plane carries what was allowed to happen.

The UE: Where the Subscriber Enters the Network

The User Equipment (UE) is the subscriber's device: a smartphone, router, modem, tablet, IoT device, or any other terminal capable of accessing the LTE network.

From an engineering perspective, however, the UE is much more than a device with an IP address. It contains identities, security context, radio capabilities, subscription information, and the logic required to interact with the LTE system.

The UE communicates with the radio access network through the LTE-Uu interface. This is the air interface between the device and the eNodeB.

A common mistake when troubleshooting LTE is to think of the UE as simply generating IP packets. Before meaningful user traffic can flow, the network must establish connectivity, authenticate the subscriber, negotiate parameters, create bearers, and install forwarding state.

An IP packet seen on the user plane is usually the final result of a much larger signaling process.

This distinction becomes extremely important later when analyzing packet captures. Seeing GTP-U traffic means that a user-plane path exists. It does not necessarily tell you why that path was created, which policy authorized it, or whether the subscriber's signaling state is healthy.

The eNodeB: More Than a Radio Tower

The eNodeB, or evolved NodeB, is the LTE radio access node. It provides the radio connection between the UE and the EPC.

In older mobile architectures, radio access and core functions were separated through additional network elements. LTE simplified the architecture by integrating much more functionality into the eNodeB.

The eNodeB communicates with the EPC through two fundamentally different logical interfaces:

S1-MME — Control-plane connection between the eNodeB and the MME.
S1-U — User-plane connection between the eNodeB and the SGW.

This separation is fundamental.

The eNodeB can exchange signaling with the MME while simultaneously forwarding user traffic toward the SGW.

The control connection uses S1AP, carried over SCTP. The user plane uses GTP-U, typically transported over UDP/IP.

UE | | LTE-Uu | eNodeB |\ | \ | \ | \ S1-MME S1-U | | S1AP GTP-U | | SCTP UDP/IP | | MME SGW

This distinction is one of the first things an engineer should verify when troubleshooting.

If S1AP signaling is failing, the problem is fundamentally different from a situation where S1AP is healthy but GTP-U packets are being dropped.

The MME: The Brain of LTE Mobility Management

The Mobility Management Entity (MME) is a control-plane function. It is one of the most important elements in the LTE architecture, but it is equally important to understand what it does not do.

The MME does not carry normal subscriber Internet traffic.

Instead, it manages signaling associated with mobility, authentication, session establishment, and control of the bearers used by the subscriber.

Among its major responsibilities are:

  • NAS signaling termination
  • Mobility management
  • Authentication coordination
  • Security procedures
  • Tracking Area management
  • Bearer management
  • Selection and control of serving gateways
  • Interaction with the HSS
  • Interaction with the eNodeB through S1AP

The MME communicates with the UE through NAS signaling, but that signaling is transported through the eNodeB using the S1AP connection toward the MME.

This creates an important layering relationship:

UE | | NAS | eNodeB | | S1AP | MME

The eNodeB transports the NAS messages but does not become the endpoint responsible for the subscriber's NAS mobility procedures.

The eNodeB provides access. The MME decides how the subscriber is managed inside the core.

The HSS: The Subscriber's Identity and Subscription Authority

The Home Subscriber Server (HSS) stores and provides subscriber-related information required by the LTE core.

The HSS is closely associated with authentication and subscription data. It can provide information such as the subscriber's identity, authentication vectors, and subscription-related parameters.

The MME communicates with the HSS through the S6a interface. S6a uses Diameter.

Diameter / S6a +-------------------+ | | | HSS | | | +---------+---------+ | | S6a | | +---------v---------+ | | | MME | | | +-------------------+

This interaction becomes especially important during initial registration and authentication.

When a subscriber attempts to access the LTE network, the MME cannot simply assume that the subscriber is valid. It needs to establish the subscriber's identity and obtain the information required to authenticate the device.

That information is obtained through the core network's subscriber database architecture.

Authentication is not an isolated event. It is the first proof that the network can trust the identity attempting to establish a session.

The SGW: The Anchor Between Radio and Core

The Serving Gateway (SGW) is primarily a user-plane element. It sits between the radio access network and the packet gateway.

The eNodeB sends subscriber user traffic toward the SGW using S1-U. The SGW then communicates toward the PGW through S5 or S8, depending on the architecture and roaming scenario.

UE | | LTE-Uu | eNodeB | | S1-U / GTP-U | SGW | | S5 / S8 / GTP-U | PGW | | IP | External Network

The SGW also plays an important role as a mobility anchor. When a UE moves between eNodeBs within the same serving gateway context, the user-plane path can be updated without requiring the entire packet session to be rebuilt from scratch.

This is one of the architectural decisions that makes mobility possible while preserving continuity of the packet session.

Mobility is not simply moving the radio connection. It is moving the path while preserving the session.

The PGW: The Gateway to External Networks

The Packet Data Network Gateway (PGW) is the gateway between the EPC and external packet networks.

This can mean the public Internet, a private corporate network, an operator service network, IMS-related connectivity, or another packet data network.

The PGW is also responsible for important functions related to the subscriber's IP connectivity.

Depending on the implementation and deployment, the PGW can participate in:

  • IP address allocation
  • Packet forwarding
  • Policy enforcement
  • Charging-related functions
  • Traffic filtering
  • Connectivity toward external packet networks

The PGW therefore represents an important boundary in the architecture.

Before the PGW, traffic is transported through the mobile packet core using telecom-specific tunneling mechanisms. Beyond the PGW, the traffic can behave much more like conventional IP traffic toward external networks.

The PGW is where the mobile subscriber's logical session becomes connectivity to the outside world.

The PCRF: Policy Before Packets

The Policy and Charging Rules Function (PCRF) provides policy decisions that influence how subscriber traffic is treated.

The PCRF does not normally forward user packets. Instead, it provides policy information to the gateway infrastructure.

One of its most important interfaces is Gx, which connects the PCRF with the PGW.

Policy decision +-------------------+ | PCRF | +---------+---------+ | | Gx / Diameter | +---------v---------+ | PGW | +---------+---------+ | | User traffic

This distinction illustrates a recurring principle in telecom architecture: the entity making a decision does not necessarily carry the traffic affected by that decision.

The PCRF can influence parameters such as QoS and charging rules, while the PGW applies the resulting policy to the subscriber's session.

A packet does not need to know the policy. The network already knows what the packet is allowed to do.

The important point is not simply remembering these names.

Every interface is a boundary. Every boundary is a possible failure domain.

Control Plane and User Plane

The separation between control plane and user plane is one of the most important concepts in mobile networking.

The control plane establishes identity, mobility state, session state, bearers, policies, and forwarding information.

The user plane carries the actual subscriber traffic.

Consider a subscriber opening a web page. The HTTP request is user-plane traffic. The signaling required to authenticate the subscriber and establish the session is control-plane traffic.

CONTROL PLANE UE ---- NAS ----> MME ---- Diameter ----> HSS | | GTP-C | v SGW | GTP-C | v PGW | Policy ^ | PCRF USER PLANE UE ========> eNodeB ========> SGW ========> PGW ========> Internet GTP-U GTP-U

The user plane is therefore dependent on the control plane.

A GTP-U tunnel does not simply appear by itself. It exists because signaling procedures established the state necessary to create and use that tunnel.

The user plane is the visible traffic. The control plane is the invisible machinery that makes that traffic possible.

GTP: The Tunnel Beneath the Session

The GPRS Tunneling Protocol (GTP) is one of the defining technologies of the LTE packet core.

For LTE engineering, two forms are especially important: GTP-C and GTP-U.

GTP-C

GTP-C is used for control signaling between EPC nodes. It is responsible for procedures associated with creating, modifying, and deleting tunnel and session state.

For example, when a subscriber establishes a PDN connection, the core needs to coordinate the creation of the corresponding session across multiple network elements.

GTP-C carries that control information.

GTP-U

GTP-U carries the actual subscriber payload. An IP packet generated by the UE can be encapsulated inside GTP-U and transported through the mobile core.

Original subscriber packet +-----------------------------+ | IP Packet | | Source: UE IP | | Destination: Internet | +-----------------------------+ becomes +-----------------------------------------------+ | Outer IP | | UDP | | GTP-U | | +-------------------------------------------+ | | | Original Subscriber IP Packet | | | +-------------------------------------------+ | +-----------------------------------------------+

This is why packet captures taken on an S1-U or S5/S8 interface can look very different from a capture taken on the Internet-facing side of the PGW.

The packet is not necessarily changed at the application level, but it is encapsulated inside a telecom-specific transport tunnel while crossing the mobile core.

When troubleshooting LTE, always ask: am I looking at the original packet, or am I looking at the tunnel carrying the packet?

TEIDs: How the Core Knows Which Tunnel Belongs to Which Session

A critical field inside GTP is the TEID — Tunnel Endpoint Identifier.

The TEID allows the receiving network element to associate a GTP packet with the appropriate tunnel and session context.

This is essential because a gateway can simultaneously handle traffic from thousands or millions of subscribers.

The outer IP addresses tell the network where the GTP packet is going. The TEID helps tell the receiving node which logical tunnel the encapsulated subscriber traffic belongs to.

SGW | +----------+----------+ | | | TEID A TEID B TEID C | | | UE-A UE-B UE-C

This is one reason TEIDs become extremely valuable during packet analysis.

If a packet capture shows a GTP-U stream, identifying the TEID and following it through the capture can reveal whether packets belonging to the same subscriber session are consistently forwarded.

How the Pieces Work Together

It is useful to stop thinking about each network element individually and reconstruct the complete logic of a subscriber session.

Imagine a UE attaching to the LTE network for the first time.

The UE establishes radio connectivity with the eNodeB. The eNodeB establishes or uses its S1 connection toward the MME. The MME receives NAS signaling. The MME contacts the HSS through S6a to obtain subscriber and authentication information.

Once authentication and mobility procedures are successfully completed, the network establishes the necessary session and bearer state.

The MME coordinates with the SGW using S11. The SGW and PGW establish the corresponding user-plane context. The PGW provides or manages the subscriber's connectivity toward the external packet network. Policy information can be obtained through the PCRF over Gx.

Finally, the eNodeB receives the necessary information to forward subscriber traffic toward the appropriate SGW tunnel.

UE | | Radio Access v eNodeB | | S1AP / NAS v MME | | S6a v HSS MME | | S11 / GTP-C v SGW | | S5/S8 / GTP-C + GTP-U v PGW | | External IP v Internet Policy path: PCRF | | Gx v PGW

The important realization is that this is not one procedure. It is a coordinated set of procedures creating distributed state across multiple network elements.

A mobile session exists because multiple nodes agree that it exists.

The Same Subscriber, Seen From Different Network Elements

A powerful way to troubleshoot LTE is to ask what each node should know about the subscriber.

The UE knows its radio and security state.

The eNodeB knows the radio-side context and the mappings required to forward traffic toward the core.

The MME knows the subscriber's mobility and control-plane state.

The HSS knows the subscriber's identity and subscription information.

The SGW knows the user-plane tunnel state toward the radio access network and the PGW.

The PGW knows the external packet-data session and its associated user-plane state.

The PCRF knows the policy that should apply to the subscriber's service.

No single element necessarily contains the entire story.

Troubleshooting becomes easier when you stop asking “which box is broken?” and start asking “which part of the subscriber's state is missing or inconsistent?”

Why a Subscriber Can Be Attached but Have No Internet

This is one of the most useful practical examples of the architecture.

Suppose the UE successfully completes LTE registration and authentication. The MME is communicating with the HSS. The eNodeB is healthy. Yet the user cannot access the Internet.

It would be a mistake to conclude that the entire LTE network is working simply because the UE is attached.

The control plane can be partially successful while the user plane is broken.

Possible causes include:

  • Incorrect SGW or PGW selection
  • Failed GTP-C session establishment
  • Missing or incorrect bearer state
  • Incorrect TEID mapping
  • GTP-U packet loss
  • Routing problems toward the external network
  • Incorrect subscriber IP configuration
  • Policy enforcement problems
  • MTU or fragmentation problems
  • Firewall or ACL issues

The architecture immediately tells us where to look.

If authentication works but user traffic does not, the investigation should move beyond simply checking the radio signal.

“Attached” does not mean “connected to the Internet.” It only means that part of the network has successfully completed its job.

Failure Domains and Engineering Thinking

The EPC architecture naturally creates multiple failure domains.

A problem on S6a can affect authentication without necessarily affecting an already-established user session.

A problem on S1-MME can prevent signaling between the eNodeB and MME.

A problem on S1-U can break subscriber traffic while control signaling remains operational.

A problem on S11 can prevent session or bearer management.

A problem on S5/S8 can break connectivity between the SGW and PGW.

A problem on Gx can affect policy while basic signaling and radio connectivity may remain healthy.

This is why interface-level thinking is so valuable.

Subscriber problem | +---- Radio? | +---- S1-MME? | +---- S6a? | +---- S11? | +---- S1-U? | +---- S5/S8? | +---- Gx / Policy? | +---- External IP network?

The architecture becomes a troubleshooting map.

Reading the Network Through Packets

Once the EPC architecture is understood, packet captures become much more meaningful.

Instead of seeing a collection of protocols, the engineer can associate each packet with a function.

  • S1AP points toward eNodeB-to-MME control signaling.
  • NAS reveals UE mobility and session signaling.
  • Diameter can reveal subscriber and policy interactions.
  • GTP-C reveals session and bearer control.
  • GTP-U reveals subscriber user traffic inside the mobile core.
  • SCTP is a key transport for S1 control signaling.

The packet capture is no longer just a technical artifact. It becomes a timeline of the network's decisions.

A good telecom engineer does not merely read packets. They reconstruct the story those packets are telling.

The Mental Model to Keep

The LTE EPC can initially look complicated because it contains many interfaces and specialized network functions.

But the architecture becomes much simpler when reduced to a few fundamental questions.

Who is the subscriber?
The HSS and associated identity and authentication procedures answer this question.

Where is the subscriber?
The MME manages mobility-related state.

How does the subscriber reach the packet network?
The SGW and PGW establish and maintain the user-plane path.

What is the subscriber allowed to do?
Policy functions such as the PCRF provide the rules that influence service behavior.

How does the traffic actually move?
GTP-U transports subscriber traffic through the mobile core.

How does the network coordinate all of this?
Control-plane protocols such as S1AP, NAS, Diameter, and GTP-C create and maintain the necessary state.

The EPC is best understood as a distributed state machine wrapped around an IP data path.

Chapter Takeaways

The most important lesson from this chapter is not the list of EPC acronyms. It is the relationship between them.

The UE accesses the LTE network through the eNodeB. The eNodeB separates control and user traffic toward the EPC. The MME manages mobility and control-plane procedures. The HSS provides subscriber and authentication information. The SGW anchors and transports user-plane traffic. The PGW provides connectivity toward external packet networks. The PCRF supplies policy decisions. And GTP creates the tunnels that allow subscriber traffic to cross the core.

The most important interfaces form a map:

UE ↔ eNodeB LTE-Uu eNodeB ↔ MME S1-MME / S1AP / SCTP eNodeB ↔ SGW S1-U / GTP-U MME ↔ SGW S11 / GTP-C SGW ↔ PGW S5 / S8 / GTP MME ↔ HSS S6a / Diameter PCRF ↔ PGW Gx / Diameter

Once this map becomes intuitive, the EPC stops being a collection of acronyms. It becomes a system whose behavior can be predicted.

And that is the real objective of architecture knowledge.

When you understand the architecture, an outage stops looking random. It starts looking like a missing state, a broken interface, a failed decision, or a packet taking the wrong path.

In the next chapter, we will move from architecture into behavior. Instead of asking what each component does, we will follow the actual signaling procedures that bring a subscriber into the network — step by step, message by message.

Architecture tells you where the components are. Call flows tell you what they are actually doing.
