Monitoring, Observability and Troubleshooting

From operational visibility to evidence-based diagnosis in modern 4G and 5G networks.

A network that cannot be observed cannot be reliably operated. A network that cannot be understood cannot be reliably troubleshot.

Modern mobile networks generate an enormous amount of information.

Every network function produces metrics.

Every application generates logs.

Every session creates state transitions.

Every packet carries information.

Every failure leaves evidence somewhere.

The challenge is not obtaining data.

The challenge is determining which data matters, how different pieces of evidence are related, and what they reveal about the actual root cause.

Troubleshooting is not the process of finding something wrong. It is the process of proving what is wrong.

Monitoring and Observability Are Not the Same Thing

Monitoring and observability are closely related, but they answer different questions.

Monitoring generally focuses on known conditions.

Is CPU utilization too high?

Is packet loss increasing?

Is a service down?

Is the number of failed sessions above a threshold?

Observability goes deeper.

It asks whether the internal state of the system can be understood from the information the system produces.

Monitoring "Is something wrong?" ``` | v ``` Metrics / Thresholds | v Alert Observability "Why is it wrong?" ``` | +---- Metrics | +---- Logs | +---- Traces | +---- Events | +---- Packet Data | v Explanation ```

Monitoring tells you that something deserves attention.

Observability helps you understand what happened.

Monitoring tells you where to look. Observability helps you understand what you are looking at.

The Three Pillars

Observability is commonly associated with three major sources of telemetry:

  • Metrics
  • Logs
  • Traces

In telecom environments, packet captures and protocol signaling provide another extremely valuable source of evidence.

Observability ``` | +--------------+--------------+ | | | v v v Metrics Logs Traces | | | +--------------+--------------+ | v Correlation Layer | +---- Packet Captures | +---- Signaling | v Root Cause ```

Metrics

Metrics are numerical measurements collected over time.

They are particularly useful for understanding trends and system health.

Typical infrastructure metrics include:

  • CPU utilization
  • Memory utilization
  • Disk usage
  • Network throughput
  • Packet rate
  • Packet drops
  • Interface errors

Telecom systems add another layer of specialized metrics.

  • Attach success rate
  • Registration success rate
  • PDU session success rate
  • Bearer establishment success rate
  • Authentication failures
  • GTP tunnel failures
  • Paging success
  • Handover success

These metrics allow an engineer to understand the behavior of the network at scale.

A single failed session is an incident. A thousand similar failures are a pattern.

KPIs and KQIs

Telecom environments traditionally rely heavily on KPIs: Key Performance Indicators.

KPIs measure specific technical aspects of network performance.

Examples include:

  • Session establishment success rate
  • Drop rate
  • Latency
  • Packet loss
  • Throughput
  • Handover success rate

KQIs, or Key Quality Indicators, move closer to the actual customer experience.

The distinction can be illustrated conceptually:

Technical Layer CPU | Packet Loss | Latency | Signaling Failures | Bearer Failures | Session Failures | v KPI | v KQI | v Customer Experience

A network can have acceptable infrastructure metrics while still delivering poor service quality.

That is why technical telemetry must eventually be connected to service-level outcomes.

The network does not exist to produce good KPIs. KPIs exist to tell us whether the network is delivering the service it was built to provide.

Logs

Logs provide detailed event information.

They often answer questions that metrics cannot.

A metric may tell you that session failures increased.

A log may tell you why.

Metric PDU Session Success Rate | v 72% | v Something failed Log SMF | +-- Session request received | +-- Policy request | +-- UPF selection | +-- PFCP response timeout | +-- Session establishment failed

The log provides a sequence of events.

That sequence is often the beginning of the investigation.

Good Logs and Bad Logs

Not all logs are equally useful.

A message such as:

ERROR: Session failed

provides little context.

A useful operational log should ideally provide information such as:

  • Timestamp
  • Component
  • Event
  • Session or transaction identifier
  • Peer
  • Error code
  • Relevant context

This becomes particularly important in distributed systems.

A log without context may tell you that something failed. A correlated log can tell you why it failed.

Traces

Distributed tracing becomes increasingly valuable in cloud-native 5G environments.

A single subscriber operation may involve multiple network functions.

UE | v gNB | v AMF | +----> AUSF | +----> UDM | v SMF | +----> PCF | v UPF

If every component produces independent logs, following one transaction across the entire system can become difficult.

Distributed tracing attempts to solve this problem by associating related operations with a common trace context.

Trace ID: ABC123 AMF | +---- Request Authentication | v AUSF | +---- Authentication Processing | v UDM | +---- Subscriber Data | v AMF | +---- Registration Continue | v SMF | +---- PDU Session | v UPF

Instead of examining isolated events, the engineer can reconstruct the journey of the transaction.

Telemetry Correlation

The real power appears when metrics, logs, traces, and packet data are correlated.

Incident ``` | v Session Success ↓ | +----------+----------+ | | | v v v Metrics Logs Traces | | | +----------+----------+ | v Packet Capture | v Correlated View | v Root Cause ```

Consider a PDU session problem.

The metric reports a sudden decrease in success rate.

The SMF log reports PFCP timeouts.

The trace shows that the SMF sent a request to the UPF but did not receive a response.

The packet capture confirms that the PFCP request leaves the SMF but no response returns.

The investigation has now moved from:

“PDU sessions are failing.”

to:

“The SMF cannot receive PFCP responses from the UPF.”

That is a dramatically better troubleshooting position.

Grafana and Visualization

Visualization is essential when dealing with large amounts of telemetry.

Grafana is widely used to build dashboards that transform raw metrics into operational views.

Grafana ``` +----------------------+ | Network Overview | +----------------------+ | CPU 62% | | Memory 71% | | Sessions 98.4% | | Latency 14 ms | +----------------------+ +----------------------+ | Session Success | | | | /\ /\ | | / \____/ \__ | +----------------------+ ```

A good dashboard should not simply display everything.

It should help the engineer answer important questions quickly.

What changed?

When did it change?

Which component changed?

What else changed at the same time?

A dashboard is not a wall of graphs. It is a decision-making interface.

Prometheus

Prometheus is widely used for metrics collection and monitoring in cloud-native environments.

It is particularly useful when combined with Kubernetes and Grafana.

Network Functions | v Metrics Endpoints | v Prometheus | v Time-Series Data | v Grafana | v Engineer

The important concept is not the tool itself.

The important concept is that operational data becomes structured, queryable, and correlated over time.

ELK and Log-Centric Observability

ELK-based environments are commonly used for centralized log collection and analysis.

Network Functions | +---- Logs | +---- Events | +---- Errors | v Log Collection | v Elasticsearch | v Kibana | v Investigation

Centralized logging becomes essential when the network contains many distributed components.

Instead of connecting to individual servers, engineers can search for events across the environment.

Splunk

Splunk provides another approach to centralized operational and security data analysis.

It can ingest logs, events, and other machine-generated data and make them searchable and correlatable.

In telecom environments, this can be especially useful when combining:

  • Network events
  • Application logs
  • Security alerts
  • Infrastructure events
  • Subscriber-related events

The goal remains the same:

Turn large volumes of raw data into evidence that supports decisions.

Alerting

Monitoring becomes operationally useful when it can identify conditions that require attention.

But alerting introduces its own engineering challenges.

An alert can be:

  • Useful
  • Informational
  • Duplicate
  • Transient
  • False positive
  • Critical

Poor alerting can overwhelm an operations team.

Healthy System | v Monitoring | v Hundreds of Alerts | v Alert Fatigue | v Critical Alert Ignored

The goal should therefore not be to generate as many alerts as possible.

The goal is to generate alerts that lead to meaningful action.

If everything is an alert, nothing is an alert.

Alert Severity

A mature monitoring strategy should distinguish between different levels of impact.

INFO | | Something changed v WARNING | | Risk is increasing v CRITICAL | | Service is impacted v INCIDENT | | Immediate action required v RESOLUTION

Severity should reflect operational impact, not simply how frightening the metric looks.

Alert Correlation

A single infrastructure failure can generate dozens or hundreds of alerts.

Suppose a network interface fails.

Interface Failure | +---- Packet Loss | +---- GTP Errors | +---- Session Failures | +---- Latency Increase | +---- Application Errors | +---- Customer Complaints

These are not necessarily six independent problems.

They may be six symptoms of one root cause.

The number of alerts does not represent the number of problems.

Correlation attempts to identify the relationship between events.

Troubleshooting Methodology

Good troubleshooting is not random experimentation.

It is a structured investigation.

Symptom | v Scope | v Timeline | v Evidence | v Hypothesis | v Validation | v Root Cause | v Resolution | v Verification

Each step has a purpose.

Start With the Symptom

The first mistake in troubleshooting is often jumping directly to a suspected cause.

For example:

“Users cannot access the internet, so the UPF is broken.”

That is not a diagnosis.

It is a hypothesis.

The actual symptom might be:

“PDU session establishment success dropped from 99.5% to 72% at 14:32.”

That statement is measurable.

It gives us a timestamp.

It gives us an impact level.

And it gives us a starting point.

A precise symptom is the first piece of evidence.

Define the Scope

The next question is:

How widespread is the problem?

Is it:

  • One subscriber?
  • One cell?
  • One region?
  • One APN/DNN?
  • One network function?
  • One interface?
  • The entire network?
Single UE | v Single Cell | v Regional | v National | v Global

The scope often eliminates entire categories of possible causes.

If only one subscriber is affected, a national core outage becomes less likely.

If every subscriber is affected, an isolated UE problem becomes unlikely.

Build the Timeline

Time is one of the most powerful dimensions in troubleshooting.

The question is not only:

“What is broken?”

It is:

“What changed immediately before the problem started?”

14:20 | | Normal | 14:28 | | Software deployment | 14:31 | | Interface errors | 14:32 | | Session success drops | 14:35 | | Customer complaints

The timeline creates relationships between events.

A deployment occurring three minutes before the incident deserves investigation.

It does not automatically prove causality.

But it is evidence.

From Symptom to Evidence

A disciplined engineer continuously converts assumptions into evidence.

Assumption | v "What could cause this?" | v Hypothesis | v "What evidence would confirm it?" | v Measurement | v Packet / Log / Metric | v Evidence

This prevents troubleshooting from becoming guesswork.

A hypothesis is useful only when it can be tested.

Control Plane Versus User Plane

One of the most powerful troubleshooting techniques in mobile networks is separating control-plane and user-plane analysis.

Service Failure | +--------+--------+ | | v v Control Plane User Plane | | v v Session Exists? Traffic Flows? | | v v Signaling OK? GTP-U OK? | | +--------+--------+ | v Correlation

If a PDU session is never established, investigating application traffic may be premature.

If the PDU session is established but user traffic does not flow, the investigation moves toward the user plane.

This separation dramatically reduces the search space.

Evidence at Multiple Layers

A strong investigation examines several layers rather than trusting a single data source.

Layer 1 Physical / NIC | v Layer 2 Ethernet | v Layer 3 IP | v Layer 4 UDP / SCTP / TCP | v Layer 5+ GTP / NAS / S1AP / HTTP/2 | v Application / Service

A failure at a lower layer can create symptoms at every layer above it.

This is why starting with the highest-level symptom and immediately blaming the application can be misleading.

Packet Capture as Evidence

Packet captures provide direct evidence of what actually crossed the network.

They can confirm:

  • Whether packets were transmitted
  • Whether packets were received
  • Which endpoint communicated
  • Which protocol was used
  • Which message sequence occurred
  • Whether retransmissions occurred
  • Whether responses were received
Expected Request | v Network | v Response Observed Request | v Network | X No Response

That simple observation can eliminate entire sections of the architecture from the investigation.

The packet capture does not care what we expected to happen. It shows what actually happened.

Wireshark and TShark

Wireshark provides deep packet inspection and protocol decoding.

TShark provides similar analysis capabilities through a command-line interface, which makes it particularly useful for automation, remote systems, and large-scale packet processing.

In telecom troubleshooting, protocol filters can dramatically reduce investigation time.

Large Capture | v Protocol Filter | +---- NAS | +---- S1AP | +---- GTP | +---- PFCP | +---- HTTP/2 | v Relevant Evidence

The goal is not to inspect every packet.

The goal is to isolate the packets that can prove or disprove the current hypothesis.

Root Cause Analysis

Finding the first failure is not always the same as finding the root cause.

Consider:

UPF | | PFCP timeout v SMF | | Session establishment fails v Subscriber | | Internet unavailable v Customer Complaint

The customer sees an internet outage.

The SMF sees a session failure.

The root cause may actually be a network path problem between SMF and UPF.

Or perhaps the UPF itself is overloaded.

Or perhaps a firewall policy changed.

The visible symptom is several layers away from the root cause.

The first error you see is not necessarily the first error that happened.

Five Whys in Telecom

A simple root-cause technique is repeatedly asking why.

Why did users lose connectivity? | v Because PDU sessions failed. | v Why did PDU sessions fail? | v Because PFCP establishment timed out. | v Why did PFCP time out? | v Because responses did not reach the SMF. | v Why? | v Network policy blocked UDP traffic. | v Why? | v Firewall rule changed during deployment.

The original symptom was:

“Users cannot access the internet.”

The root cause was:

“A firewall policy change blocked PFCP traffic.”

Those are very different statements.

Root Cause Versus Contributing Factors

Complex incidents rarely have only one factor.

For example:

Primary Cause | v Firewall Policy Change | +---- PFCP Blocked | +---- Sessions Fail | +---- Retries Increase | +---- SMF CPU Increases | +---- Alerts Trigger

The CPU increase is real.

The session failures are real.

The alerts are real.

But they may all be consequences of the original event.

Root-cause analysis means separating causes from consequences.

Observability for 5G Core

The cloud-native architecture of the 5G Core makes observability increasingly important.

Network functions can be distributed across:

  • Virtual machines
  • Containers
  • Kubernetes clusters
  • Multiple nodes
  • Multiple availability zones
  • Edge locations
5G Core ``` Kubernetes | +-------------+-------------+ | | | v v v AMF SMF PCF | | | +-------------+-------------+ | UPF | User Plane ```

A service failure can therefore involve application behavior, Kubernetes behavior, networking, infrastructure, and telecom signaling simultaneously.

This is why modern telecom troubleshooting increasingly requires both telecom and cloud-native observability skills.

Observability Across the Stack

Customer Experience | v KQI | v KPI | v Application | v Network Function | v Container / Pod | v Kubernetes | v CNI / Network | v Node / OS | v NIC / Hardware

A senior engineer must be able to move up and down this stack.

If the application says the network is unavailable, look downward.

If the infrastructure is healthy but the service is failing, look upward.

If everything appears healthy individually but the transaction fails, investigate the interaction between components.

Distributed systems often fail between components rather than inside components.

Correlation IDs and Transaction Context

Correlation becomes particularly powerful when systems share identifiers.

Examples include:

  • Trace IDs
  • Session IDs
  • TEIDs
  • Transaction identifiers
  • Subscriber-related identifiers

These identifiers allow engineers to follow an event across different systems.

Subscriber | +---- Session ID | +---- AMF Log | +---- SMF Log | +---- UPF State | +---- GTP Tunnel | +---- Packet Capture | v One Story

Without correlation, each system tells a different fragment of the story.

With correlation, those fragments can become one coherent timeline.

Observability is the art of turning fragments into a story.

The engineer should be able to move from a high-level symptom toward increasingly detailed evidence.

This is the difference between troubleshooting and guessing.

Evidence-Based Troubleshooting

A mature troubleshooting process continuously asks:

What do I know?

What do I think?

What evidence separates the two?

KNOWN | | Facts v UNKNOWN | | Hypotheses v TEST | | Evidence v CONFIRMED | v ROOT CAUSE

This approach prevents engineers from becoming emotionally attached to their first theory.

The first hypothesis can be wrong.

That is normal.

The important thing is to discover that it is wrong quickly.

The best troubleshooting engineers are not the ones who guess the cause fastest. They are the ones who eliminate wrong causes fastest.

Automation and Observability

Modern networks produce too much information to investigate everything manually.

Automation can help identify patterns.

Examples include:

  • Automatic anomaly detection
  • Log correlation
  • Threshold evaluation
  • Packet analysis pipelines
  • Automated dashboards
  • Incident enrichment
  • Alert deduplication

A mature operational platform can automatically collect evidence before an engineer even starts the investigation.

Incident | v Alert | v Automatic Enrichment | +---- Metrics +---- Logs +---- Traces +---- Events +---- Packet Data | v Investigation Package | v Engineer

This dramatically reduces mean time to investigate.

MTTD and MTTR

Operational teams often measure two important concepts:

Mean Time to Detect and Mean Time to Recover.

Failure | |---- MTTD ----| v v Detected Investigation | |---- Diagnosis | |---- Fix | v Recovery

Better observability reduces the time between failure and detection.

Better troubleshooting reduces the time between detection and diagnosis.

Better automation can reduce both.

The value of observability is not measured by how many graphs exist. It is measured by how quickly the team can understand and recover from failure.

The same telemetry used for troubleshooting can also support security.

An unusual signaling pattern may be:

  • A network fault
  • A configuration error
  • A capacity problem
  • Or an attack

Without observability, these possibilities are difficult to distinguish.

Unexpected Behavior | +----------------+ | | v v Operational Issue Security Issue | | +-------+--------+ | v Evidence | v Diagnosis

Security monitoring depends on observability.

Troubleshooting depends on observability.

Reliability depends on observability.

The Senior Engineer's Perspective

A junior engineer may start with the component they know best.

A senior engineer starts with the behavior of the system.

The question is not:

“Is the UPF healthy?”

The better question is:

“Is the service working, and what evidence tells us why or why not?”

A component can report itself as healthy while the service it participates in is failing.

Component health is not the same as service health.

This distinction becomes critical in distributed telecom architectures.

The Troubleshooting Discipline

A disciplined engineer avoids several common mistakes.

Do not change multiple things simultaneously.

Do not assume the first suspected component is responsible.

Do not ignore timestamps.

Do not rely on a single monitoring system.

Do not confuse symptoms with causes.

Do not close an incident merely because the service recovered.

And most importantly:

Do not stop when you find something unusual. Determine whether it explains the failure.

Finding something wrong is easy. Proving that it caused the incident is engineering.

Closing Perspective

Modern telecom networks are too distributed and too complex to operate through intuition alone.

The engineer needs visibility.

Metrics show behavior over time.

Logs reveal events.

Traces reveal distributed transactions.

Packet captures reveal what actually crossed the network.

KPIs measure technical performance.

KQIs connect that performance to service quality.

Dashboards make information understandable.

Alerting brings important changes to the engineer's attention.

Correlation turns isolated events into a coherent picture.

And methodology transforms that picture into a diagnosis.

This is the mindset required to operate complex 4G and 5G networks.

Good engineers fix problems. Great engineers build systems that make problems visible, explainable, and recoverable.
From symptom to evidence. From evidence to hypothesis. From hypothesis to proof. From proof to root cause.

That is the foundation of reliable telecom operations.

And as mobile networks continue to evolve toward cloud-native, distributed, automated architectures, the ability to observe and reason about the entire system becomes just as important as the ability to configure it.
